How many aircraft one person can supervise, and how a robot team should split a task.

Current

Supervising multiple uncrewed aircraft

I am a PhD candidate in the Human-Machine Teaming Laboratory, advised by Julie Adams. With Christopher Sanchez, the lab asks how many uncrewed aircraft one remote pilot can be responsible for in a high-stakes setting. The case in front of the team now is wildland fire: large aircraft supporting the fire, a flight simulator, and a remote-pilot interface. I am on the current team, with Alejandra Hilbert and Atharv Marathe. Andrew Dassonville built the pilot interface and the experimental systems.

Oregon State Engineering posted the setup.

The published baseline is a different interface, Wing’s delivery display. I am a coauthor on that technical report. Pilots stayed in a normal workload range and kept good situation awareness as the number of aircraft and nests changed. The open measurement problem, across this line of work, is estimating workload from objective signals such as heart-rate variability, speech rate, and noise level while the task is still underway. Sponsors of the adaptive teaming work include DARPA/NASA and AFOSR.

Wing baseline report HRI 2024 companion, design principles for the models

Earlier

Georgia Tech, STAR Lab, with Harish Ravichandar. 2021–2023.

  1. 2023

    Trait preferences

    First author. Expert demonstrations show which traits matter because the expert assigns the important ones consistently. Using those preferences made allocation more effective than treating every trait as equal, and let the optimizer drop traits. Sponsored by ARL.

    AAMAS extended abstract

  2. 2023

    Unknown task rewards

    Coauthor. When the reward of each task is unknown and the robots are limited, the algorithm has to try new coalitions without abandoning ones that already work.

    RSS 2023

Also at Georgia Tech: trainability feedback inside decentralized allocation, a StarCraft II demonstration study for a Gaussian-process allocator, and a personalized embedding for pose prediction. Those did not become papers.
